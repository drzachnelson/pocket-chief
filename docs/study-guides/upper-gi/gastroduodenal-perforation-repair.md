# Gastroduodenal Perforation Repair

**Draft — pending Zach's clinical review**
Prepared: **2026-10-22** · Slug: `gastroduodenal-perforation-repair`
SCORE: **Stomach → Operations/Procedures → Gastroduodenal Perforation Repair**

Companions: [PUD](peptic-ulcer-disease.md), [vagotomy](vagotomy-and-drainage.md), [UGIB](upper-gastrointestinal-bleeding.md), [EGD](esophagogastroduodenoscopy.md). This guide addresses peptic perforation; an iatrogenic endoscopic perforation requires a defect- and contamination-specific assessment.

## At a glance

**Physiology, contamination, ulcer site, and tissue quality determine the operation.** Resuscitate and treat sepsis while arranging source control. A small anterior duodenal defect often permits a limited closure/omental repair. A gastric ulcer needs malignancy assessment. A large friable duodenal defect may require a more complex reconstruction or decompressive strategy. Definitive acid-reducing surgery is a selective addition for a stable patient, not the first objective in shock. [S3 §§1,4–6; S2 pp.399–401]

## Protective anatomy

| Structure | Why it changes the repair |
|---|---|
| Lesser curvature | Right/left gastric vessels; ulcer excision must preserve an adequate perfused remnant. [S3 §1] |
| Greater curvature and fundus | Gastroepiploic and short-gastric supply; preserve blood supply when mobilizing stomach or omentum. [S3 §1] |
| Duodenal vascular arcade | Superior pancreaticoduodenal supply from the GDA and inferior supply from the SMA; inflow is interconnected. [S3 §1] |
| Posterior duodenum, bile duct, ampulla | Resection or deep sutures in inflamed tissue risk duct/ampullary injury; identify the ampulla before a major duodenal resection. [S3 §6; S2 pp.398–399] |
| Hiatus and vagal trunks | Left vagus anterior, right posterior; relevant if an acid-reducing procedure is being considered. [S3 §1; S4 §2.2] |

Most duodenal ulcers occur in the first portion: **anterior ulcers tend to perforate; posterior ulcers tend to bleed.** Gastric perforations often involve the anterior lesser-curve region. Posterior leakage may be contained in the lesser sac. [S3 §§1,3; S2 p.398]

## Causes and presentation

H. pylori and NSAIDs are major drivers; review smoking, steroids, recurrent ulcer history, acid hypersecretion, and possible malignancy. A repaired hole is not a completed treatment plan if the underlying ulcer cause remains. [S3 §§2,8]

Sudden severe epigastric pain may progress to generalized peritonitis, fever, and tachycardia. Pain may be localized with a contained leak or referred toward the shoulder/subphrenic region. Older, immunosuppressed, or already hospitalized patients may have less dramatic symptoms; deterioration and peritoneal signs matter more than a classic story. [S3 §3; S1 §7]

## Establish the diagnosis without delaying source control

CT is more sensitive than a plain film for a small perforation and can show extraluminal air, free/contained fluid, wall thickening, or a defect. An upright chest film may identify subdiaphragmatic air, but absence of free air does not exclude perforation. Water-soluble contrast imaging can help assess a suspected sealed leak when the initial study is inconclusive and the patient is stable. [S3 §3; S10 Perforated peptic ulcer—Diagnosis]

**Do not make a deteriorating patient wait for an elaborate localization study.** Diffuse peritonitis, sepsis, free spillage, and hemodynamic instability are operative triggers. The goal is timely source control with simultaneous resuscitation. [S3 §4]

## Resuscitation and preparation

1. Keep NPO; obtain IV access, correct volume depletion and electrolyte abnormalities, and monitor hemodynamics. Vomiting and reduced intake commonly leave substantial deficits. [S3 §4]
2. Give IV PPI and broad-spectrum antibacterial treatment covering enteric gram-negative organisms and anaerobes; account for local resistance and clinical context. [S3 §§4,7]
3. Arrange gastric decompression and operative access while evaluating the safest approach. A severely unstable patient needs an operation that can achieve control promptly. [S3 §§5–7]
4. Do not add routine empiric antifungal treatment solely because this is a perforated ulcer. Consider it selectively for high-risk patients or microbiologic findings. This differs from the unqualified yeast coverage in the supplied Fiser text. [S3 §4; S10 Antimicrobial therapy]

The precise drug, dose, renal adjustment, and duration are not standardized by this guide. After adequate source control, WSES suggests a short antibacterial course of approximately **3–5 days**, with reassessment if infection persists. [S10 Antimicrobial therapy]

## Who can be observed without an operation

Nonoperative care is an exception for a stable, nonperitonitic patient with a demonstrably sealed/contained perforation and no active contrast extravasation. It requires bowel rest, IV fluids, PPI, antibiotics, serial examination and monitoring, and immediate ability to operate if the condition worsens. A contained collection on one scan is not enough if physiology is deteriorating. [S3 §4; S1 §7]

**Failure signals:** worsening pain or peritonitis, instability, sepsis, or ongoing leakage. Escalate to operative source control rather than merely extending fasting. The supplied text acknowledges no formal reimaging schedule; its proposed 2–7-day fasting interval is expert practice, not a universal rule. [S3 §4]

## Choose the repair by site size and tissue

| Finding | Practical operative direction |
|---|---|
| Small duodenal defect with usable surrounding tissue | Primary repair with or without omental reinforcement, chosen by tissue quality; avoid sutures that tear through the edge. [S3 §6; S10 Surgical treatment—small perforations] |
| Large/friable duodenal defect, approximately 2 cm or greater | Tailor repair or reconstruction; evaluate ampulla, duodenal viability, decompression, and feeding access. A patch is not automatically durable. [S3 §6; S10 Surgical treatment—large perforations] |
| Gastric ulcer in an unstable patient | Limited closure/omental repair or wedge excision for source control, with malignancy sampling when safe. [S3 §5] |
| Large gastric ulcer or repair that would narrow pylorus/GE junction | Formal resection/reconstruction may be required if physiology permits. [S3 §5] |
| Stable patient with genuinely refractory high-acid disease | Consider added acid reduction after evaluating the specific indication and operative burden. [S3 §5; S4 §1] |

The source's small/large categories leave the exact 2-cm boundary imprecise. Size is a warning about failure risk, not a substitute for examining tissue, contamination, anatomy, and physiology. [S3 §6; editorial synthesis]

## Small duodenal perforation: operative sequence

1. **Expose and identify the defect.** Assess site, contamination, adjacent tissue, and whether the presumed duodenal ulcer is actually gastric or a different lesion. [S3 §§5–6]
2. **Assess closure quality.** Friable edges can split with suturing; avoid enlarging the opening through repeated attempts at a tight primary closure. [S3 §6]
3. **Bring vascularized omentum without tension.** Use a viable tongue to cover/reinforce the defect, securing sutures in tissue that can hold. Primary closure before patching is selective, not compulsory. [S3 §6]
4. **Address contamination and decompression.** Irrigate, consider drains according to defect/repair risk, and decompress the stomach. SCORE describes wide drainage; routine drainage for every low-risk repair is not established by the supplied comparison evidence. [S3 §§5–7]
5. **Plan enteral access when needed.** A large or complex repair may justify feeding jejunostomy; the source's broad advice to obtain access is individualized rather than an instruction to place a tube in every small repair. [S3 §6]

**Name the construction, not just the eponym.** The PUD module distinguishes a free omental Graham plug from a pedicled modified Graham repair, while the procedure module calls a vascularized tongue a Graham patch. Describe primary closure versus patch-only, tissue viability, and fixation in the operative reasoning. [S1 §7; S3 §6]

## Large duodenal defects and difficult rescue

Available options include an omental/falciform plug, a jejunal serosal patch, resection with reconstruction, or diversion/decompression. None is a universal next step. The ampulla and inflamed duodenal stump make aggressive resection hazardous; stabilize the patient and choose a repair that the tissue can support. [S3 §6]

| Technique | Purpose and limitation |
|---|---|
| Jejunal serosal patch, often called Thal patch | Cover a duodenal defect with an adjacent jejunal serosal surface; requires an appropriate local anatomy and viable tissue. [S3 §6] |
| Triple-tube approach | Gastrostomy and retrograde jejunostomy decompress; antegrade jejunostomy feeds. This is a selected protection strategy, not routine repair. [S3 §6] |
| Gastrojejunostomy with/without pyloric exclusion | Diverts flow away from a difficult duodenal repair; exclusion is temporary. [S3 §6] |
| Resection/reconstruction | May be necessary when local repair is unsuitable; balance disease removal against duct injury, stump leak, and physiologic cost. [S3 §6] |

A patient in shock may need an abbreviated source-control operation. A definitive ulcer operation or elaborate reconstruction should not be added merely to satisfy a historical procedure label. [S3 §5; S10 Damage control surgery]

## Gastric perforation and reconstruction

**Malignancy assessment is essential.** Obtain ulcer tissue when safe, review the pathology, and arrange interval endoscopic evaluation if definitive sampling was not possible or results are uncertain. A patch that stops contamination does not rule out gastric cancer. [S3 §§5,7; S1 §§5–7]

Distal ulcers may permit antrectomy. Billroth I restores gastroduodenal continuity, Billroth II uses a gastrojejunostomy, and Roux-en-Y is another reconstructive option. Duodenal fibrosis/inflammation can prevent a safe Billroth I. Proximal ulcers near the GE junction may make wedge excision deforming and sometimes require more extensive individualized reconstruction. [S3 §5; S1 §6.2]

Do not convert “gastric ulcer needs biopsy” into “every unstable patient needs immediate oncologic gastrectomy.” Control contamination, preserve viable anatomy, and stage additional treatment according to physiology and pathology. [S3 §§5,7; editorial synthesis]

## Postoperative assessment and leak rescue

Initial care includes decompression/bowel rest as indicated, IV PPI, treatment of contamination, and reassessment of fluid/electrolyte and nutritional needs. Follow the clinical course and any drain output. SCORE describes contrast CT or an upper GI study before oral intake as an option; the guide does not impose a universal imaging or feeding day. [S3 §7]

New tachycardia, fever, pain/peritonitis, intolerance of intake, or suspicious drain output should prompt evaluation for leak, bleeding, or uncontrolled infection. The exact CT/drainage/reoperation algorithm requires a contemporary complication-specific source and is flagged rather than inferred from the chapter's complication list. [S3 §§7–8; S7 §7; review limit]

Review all operative pathology. Treat H. pylori with the [current regimen and cure-confirmation plan](peptic-ulcer-disease.md#treat-h-pylori-and-document-cure), stop avoidable NSAIDs, and address nicotine use. Consider ongoing acid protection when the ulcerogenic exposure cannot be removed. Recurrent disease warrants evaluation for persistent H. pylori, NSAIDs, malignancy, or hypersecretion. [S3 §§7–8]

## Late consequences worth anticipating

After resection or vagotomy, consider dumping, delayed emptying, diarrhea, alkaline reflux, nutritional deficiencies, marginal ulceration, and afferent-loop problems. The exact reconstruction predicts the complication: keep the operative anatomy in the handoff, rather than describing every procedure as a “partial gastrectomy.” The [PUD syndrome table](peptic-ulcer-disease.md#recognize-postoperative-syndromes) and [vagotomy rescue table](vagotomy-and-drainage.md#late-syndromes-and-recurrent-ulcer) provide the differential. [S3 §8; S1 §8]

## Board pearls

- **No free air does not exclude perforation.** CT is more informative for a small defect. [S3 §3]
- Nonoperative management requires a sealed leak **and** stable physiology without peritonitis. [S3 §4]
- Gastric perforation needs a malignancy plan; duodenal repair needs an ampullary/bile-duct safety plan. [S3 §§5–6]
- Omental-patch names vary across the supplied modules. Describe what is done. [S1 §7; S3 §6]
- Prior PPI use alone does not mandate a vagotomy during an emergency repair. [S2 pp.399–400 versus S3 §5]
- For a giant duodenal ulcer, the next decision is safe source control and anatomy, not simply a larger patch. [S3 §6]
