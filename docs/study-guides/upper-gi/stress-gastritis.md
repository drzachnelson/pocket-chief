## At a glance

Stress-related mucosal disease follows major physiologic illness and impaired gastric mucosal defense. **Mucosal injury is common in critical illness; clinically important bleeding is much less common.** Restore perfusion and provide appropriate enteral nutrition, assess bleeding risk, and use prophylaxis when indicated. Once significant bleeding occurs, treat it as UGIB rather than assuming a prophylactic dose of acid suppression is sufficient. [S6 §§1–3,5–6; S2 p.402]

## Mechanism and classic associations

Reduced mucosal blood flow, mucus, and bicarbonate weaken the barrier against luminal acid. Hypoxia, shock, sepsis, trauma, and organ failure therefore promote injury even without a primary hypersecretory disorder. Head injury may additionally increase acid stimulation. “Stress” here means critical physiologic illness, not ordinary psychological stress. [S6 §§1–2]

**Cushing ulcer** is the classic head-injury association; **Curling ulcer** is associated with major burns, classically duodenal. These associations help recognize the source context but do not substitute for evaluating the lesion or the patient's actual bleeding risk. [S2 p.401; S6 §1]

## Risk factors: source teaching versus prophylaxis decisions

| Context | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Shock, hypotension, sepsis, organ failure | Impaired perfusion and mucosal defense are central mechanisms. [S6 §§1–2] |
| Coagulopathy | A major listed risk factor; worsens the consequence of mucosal injury. [S6 §1.2] |
| Prolonged ventilation, classically >48 hours | A familiar board-source risk factor; current guidance does not establish ventilation alone as sufficient. [S6 §1.2; S11 Risk factors] |
| Neurocritical illness | A separate prophylaxis population in the contemporary guidance. [S11 SUP in neurocritical care] |
| Major burns, major trauma, transfusion burden, hepatic/renal dysfunction | Important clinical context from SCORE; assess the complete illness rather than giving prophylaxis to everyone with one historical association. [S6 §1] |
| Prior ulcer/bleeding, H. pylori, smoking or ulcerogenic drugs | May signal ordinary peptic disease in addition to stress injury. [S6 §1.1; S1 §3] |

The local source's incidence ranges and burn/transfusion cutoffs are historical teaching context, not a validated bedside calculator. Keep the distinction between **visible/occult blood**, **overt bleeding**, and **bleeding with physiologic or transfusion consequences**. [S6 §1; S11 Abstract/definitions]

## Prevention pathway

**Current-practice addition, SCCM/ASHP 2024:** consider critically ill adults with **coagulopathy, shock, or chronic liver disease** at risk. Use a **PPI or H2-receptor antagonist** at a low-dose prophylactic regimen; enteral or IV administration is acceptable. Provide enteral nutrition when appropriate. Enteral feeding does not remove the prophylaxis indication in a patient who remains high risk; avoid routine prophylaxis in enterally fed low-risk patients. Neurocritical patients are a separate conditional indication. Stop stress prophylaxis when the relevant risk resolves and reconcile it before ICU transfer, preserving another genuine treatment indication if present. Mechanical ventilation alone has insufficient evidence as an independent trigger. [S11 Recommendations: risk factors, SUP, EN, first-line agents, route, cessation]

This pathway should be documented as a daily indication: why prophylaxis is needed now, which agent/route is appropriate, and what would allow it to stop. Do not turn an ICU standing order into indefinite outpatient acid suppression. [S11 Cessation; editorial workflow synthesis]

## Compare prevention tools

| Tool | Mechanism/use | Important distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Enteral nutrition | Supports mucosal protection and reduces stress-ulcer risk. [S6 §5] | A prevention measure, not treatment of an uncontrolled bleed. [S6 §§5–6; editorial synthesis] |
| PPI | Inhibits parietal H+/K+-ATPase. [S6 §5] | Prophylaxis and post-hemostasis high-dose treatment are different regimens. [S11 Dosing; S12 Postendoscopic PPI] |
| H2 blocker | Reduces histamine-mediated parietal stimulation. [S6 §5] | A contemporary first-line prophylaxis option alongside PPI. [S11 First-line agents] |
| Sucralfate | Coats/protects mucosa. [S6 §5] | Listed in the source; it is not the guideline's preferred first-line class. [S11 First-line agents] |
| Antacids | Neutralize acid already in the lumen. [S6 §5] | Do not confuse neutralization with definitive hemostasis. [S6 §§5–6; editorial synthesis] |
| Prostanoids | Reduce acid stimulation/protective imbalance in source teaching. [S6 §5] | Listed mechanism, not a default ICU prophylaxis prescription. [S6 §5; S11 First-line agents] |

Exact prophylaxis doses and renal adjustments must follow the selected drug and local formulary. The guideline defines low-dose ceilings, but this guide does not substitute a single dose for the full drug-specific assessment. [S11 Dosing Regimen]

## Recognize clinically important bleeding

Presentation ranges from no symptoms or a small amount of blood in a gastric tube to hematemesis, anemia, or hemodynamic compromise. A declining hemoglobin warrants a broader bleeding/illness assessment; stress gastritis is not established by a positive stool test or the fact that a patient is in the ICU. [S6 §3.1; S5 §§2–4]

For overt or consequential bleeding, assess perfusion, airway/aspiration risk, transfusion requirements, medication exposures, and other upper sources. Use the [UGIB resuscitation pathway](upper-gastrointestinal-bleeding.md#resuscitation-before-localization). A prophylaxis indication should not distract from a new ordinary ulcer, varix, tear, or postoperative source. [S5 §§2–4,7; S6 §§3,6]

## Endoscopy and histology

Early stress lesions may appear within the first day as multiple shallow erosions, focal erythema and hemorrhage, often beginning in the fundus and progressing distally. Later lesions can show organized clot/exudate and a healing-mucosa appearance. Fiser describes clinical stress gastritis several days after the precipitating event, which need not contradict earlier microscopic/endoscopic injury. [S6 §3.2; S2 p.402]

Endoscopy identifies the mucosal pattern and can treat a focal bleeding point. If tissue is obtained, SCORE describes friability, coagulation necrosis and lymphocyte infiltration of the lamina propria. Its wording “endoscopy with biopsy is required” is not adopted as a requirement to biopsy every actively bleeding erosion; the question is whether tissue would change diagnosis and can be sampled safely. Barium studies are not the diagnostic pathway for acute mucosal bleeding. [S6 §3; S7 §6; editorial qualification]

## Classification: keep two systems separate

Stress injury is often diffuse and superficial. The **modified Johnson classification** instead describes gastric peptic-ulcer location and acid association; it does not grade stress-injury severity. This SCORE objective is covered by the inspected [PUD Johnson table](peptic-ulcer-disease.md#modified-johnson-classification), including types I–V. The actual Johnson figure embedded in the source export was not inspected. [S6 §4; S1 §4; S2 p.401]

## Treatment after a bleed and the rare operation

Manage significant bleeding with resuscitation, diagnostic/therapeutic EGD, and lesion-directed hemostasis where feasible. Focal cautery may help a discrete point; diffuse injury may not provide one target. Correct the precipitating perfusion/critical-illness problem and use treatment-level acid suppression according to the bleeding findings. [S6 §6; S2 p.402; S5 §7]

If bleeding persists or recurs, reassess for an alternative focal source and escalate through the [UGIB rescue pathway](upper-gastrointestinal-bleeding.md#rescue-after-persistent-or-recurrent-bleeding). Surgery is reserved for failure of control, instability/ongoing transfusion consequences, or perforation. A [perforation](gastroduodenal-perforation-repair.md#choose-the-repair-by-site-size-and-tissue) needs source control; a prophylaxis drug does not close a defect. [S6 §6; S5 §7; S3 §§4–6]

The supplied stress module delegates operative choice to the ulcer/bleeding procedures. It does not establish one mandatory operation for diffuse hemorrhagic gastritis, and no total-gastrectomy rule is invented here. [S6 §6]

## Board pearls

- **Mucosal hypoperfusion and failed defense** are central to stress injury. [S6 §2]
- Cushing: head injury; Curling: burns. [S2 p.401]
- The older ventilation >48-hour trigger is a board-source association; current prophylaxis considers the complete risk context. [S6 §1.2; S11 Risk factors]
- Enteral nutrition and appropriate acid prophylaxis are prevention measures; established hemorrhage needs a hemostasis pathway. [S6 §§5–6]
- A Johnson ulcer type is not a stress-gastritis grade. [S6 §4; S1 §4]
- Reconcile the prophylaxis indication when critical-illness risk resolves. [S11 Cessation]

- **Clinical approval pending.** No current-practice addition is represented as a supplied bank key.
- Source timing differs: early mucosal lesions in SCORE versus a later clinical window in Fiser. These are presented as different observed endpoints rather than one invented timing rule.
- Ventilation-alone prophylaxis, universal biopsy during active erosive bleeding, and historical incidence/cutoffs are qualified. Modern prophylaxis is separately attributed to SCCM/ASHP.
- **Needs further support:** a drug/renal-function-specific prophylaxis order set, a complete diffuse-hemorrhagic-gastritis surgical rescue strategy, and an indication-specific repeat-EGD protocol.
