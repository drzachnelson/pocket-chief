This guide covers safe diagnostic examination and major therapeutic decisions; it is not a device-specific training manual or complete sedation protocol.

## At a glance

EGD examines the esophagus, stomach, and duodenum and can diagnose, obtain tissue, and treat during the same procedure. **A useful examination combines safe access, systematic inspection, accurate landmarks, targeted therapy, and a recovery/follow-up plan.** In bleeding, restore physiology and make the airway plan before instrumentation. Afterward, new pain, tachycardia, respiratory changes, or peritoneal signs must not be dismissed as routine insufflation discomfort. [S7 §§1,4–7; S5 §3]

## Indications and what EGD can accomplish

| Problem | Role of EGD |
|---|---|
| UGIB or unexplained iron-deficiency anemia | Locate a lesion, assess stigmata, biopsy when appropriate, and obtain hemostasis; anemia evaluation may also require colonoscopy. [S7 §2] |
| Dysphagia, reflux complications, persistent vomiting | Define mucosal/structural disease and obtain tissue; selected strictures can be dilated. [S7 §2] |
| Suspected ulcer or neoplasm | Visualize, biopsy, document location and plan pathology/healing follow-up. [S7 §§2,6; S1 §5.2] |
| Foreign body or food impaction | Diagnostic/therapeutic access; object and location determine urgency and technique. [S7 §§2–3; S16 p.367] |
| Varices | Identify and treat selected bleeding/at-risk lesions, typically with banding for esophageal varices in the appropriate pathway. [S7 §2; S5 §7] |
| Nutritional access or altered anatomy | PEG planning/placement or assessment of a reconstruction, with anatomy-specific caution. [S7 §2] |
| Surveillance or caustic injury | An indication-specific examination rather than an automatic schedule inferred from this general procedure guide. [S7 §2] |

The module's broad suggestion to screen all asymptomatic reflux/sleeve patients is not adopted as universal screening advice. The indication needs disease-specific guidance, risk context, and an explicit surveillance plan. [S7 §2; review limit]

## Contraindications are tied to the planned intervention

Known/suspected perforation, inability to tolerate the intended sedation, severe physiologic instability, uncorrected coagulopathy/thrombocytopenia, and recent foregut surgery require reassessment of indication, technique, and setting. Diagnostic EGD/biopsy and therapeutic dilation/hemostasis have different bleeding/perforation risks. A suspected perforation is not a reason for an unplanned diagnostic insufflation exam, although expert endoscopy can itself be a selected perforation therapy. [S7 §§2,4,7]

**Avoid blanket rules:** coagulopathy is an important risk assessment, not an instruction to delay all lifesaving hemorrhage control until an arbitrary normal INR. Recent anastomoses and connective-tissue disorders need extra caution. The module's phrase “Whipple disease” in a list of operations is an apparent terminology error, likely referring to pancreaticoduodenectomy; do not propagate it as a contraindicating disease. [S7 §2; S9 Table 1 p.905]

## Landmarks and orientation

| Segment | What to identify |
|---|---|
| Oropharynx and inlet | Epiglottis, vocal cords, pyriform region and the esophageal inlet; enter under vision and recognize a possible diverticular opening. [S7 §§3,5] |
| Proximal esophagus | UES near 15–18 cm from incisors; this region can be difficult to evaluate and is vulnerable to instrumentation injury. [S7 §§3,5; S16 p.365] |
| Esophageal body | Smooth pale/pink squamous mucosa; note erosions, rings, webs, strictures, varices, ulcers and diverticula. [S7 §3] |
| Z-line | Squamocolumnar mucosal junction; **not automatically identical to the anatomic GE junction or diaphragmatic pinch**. [S7 §5; S2 p.391] |
| Anatomic GE junction | Proximal extent of gastric folds; compare with the diaphragmatic pinch when assessing a hiatal hernia. [S2 p.391; S7 §5] |
| Stomach | Rugal body, incisura separating body/antrum, smoother antrum, pylorus; retroflex to inspect cardia/fundus. [S7 §§3,5] |
| Duodenal bulb and D2 | Inspect bulb for ulceration; the descending duodenum has circumferential folds and the medial ampullary region. [S7 §§3,5; S5 §5.1] |

The source's clock-face orientation is position-dependent; use landmarks rather than assuming a fixed view after rotation. Fiser gives a typical distal esophageal distance around 40–42 cm, but measurements vary. Record measured landmarks rather than forcing the examination to fit a nominal distance. [S7 §§3,5; S16 pp.365–366; editorial synthesis]

Foreign bodies may lodge at the UES, the aortic/bronchial crossing, or the distal sphincter/diaphragmatic region. The size and shape of an object also affect pyloric passage. These are localization clues; a dedicated retrieval guideline is still needed for object-specific urgency and technique. [S7 §3; S16 p.365]

## Preprocedure readiness

Confirm the question, intended diagnostic/therapeutic scope, relevant prior surgery and reports, consent, allergies, medication exposure, airway/sedation history, aspiration risk, and availability of the equipment and expertise needed for the planned intervention. Previous reconstruction changes what can be reached and how much force is safe. [S7 §§2,4,6]

Selected patients may tolerate unsedated examination using a small-caliber scope and topical anesthesia. Diagnostic and uncomplicated therapeutic examinations often use moderate sedation, while patient risk, procedural complexity and airway needs may justify deeper anesthesia. The team must match the setting and rescue capability to the intended sedation rather than assuming every EGD needs the same regimen. [S7 §4]

Select laboratory testing using the clinical problem: active bleeding/anemia may require CBC and coagulation studies; renal/hepatic/metabolic disease may require chemistry testing; pregnancy and cardiopulmonary evaluation are indication-specific. Routine laboratory panels, chest films and ECGs are not required for every uncomplicated diagnostic procedure. [S7 §4]

Healthy elective fasting intervals in SCORE are **2 hours for clear liquids, 6 for a light meal, and 8 after a heavy meal**. These are not proof of an empty stomach with obstruction, gastroparesis or active bleeding. Assess the actual aspiration risk and urgency with anesthesia. [S7 §4]

**Current GLP-1 context:** the 2024 multisociety guidance permits most patients to continue treatment before elective procedures; those at higher GI/emptying risk may need a 24-hour liquid diet, an adjusted anesthesia plan or individualized delay. Do not turn this into a blanket stop-drug rule. The complete current local anesthesia protocol should govern implementation. [S14 Guidance summary]

## Antithrombotics and antibiotic decisions

Diagnostic EGD with/without mucosal biopsy is a low-bleeding-risk category in the module. Dilation, variceal ligation and PEG placement carry higher procedural risk. Balance the intended intervention against thrombotic risk, especially a recent coronary stent; involve the managing clinician before interrupting an essential antiplatelet agent. [S7 §4]

ACG-CAG's elective guidance distinguishes dual antiplatelet therapy, aspirin monotherapy, warfarin and DOACs rather than providing one “hold blood thinners” rule. Do not copy the module's clopidogrel/prasugrel/ticagrelor intervals into a universal stop/restart order without confirming agent, procedure and cardiovascular context. Acute bleeding has a separate medication pathway. [S13 Periendoscopic period; S7 §4]

Routine diagnostic EGD does not generally need antibiotic prophylaxis. PEG placement and cirrhosis with acute bleeding are important indication-specific exceptions. A prosthetic joint, pacemaker, cardiac stent or prosthetic valve alone is not a routine reason to add antibiotics for a standard diagnostic EGD. Severe neutropenia/high-bacteremia-risk procedures need individualized assessment. [S7 §4]

The source includes other endoscopic procedures (ERCP/EUS) in its antibiotic list; these are not interchangeable with EGD. Its broad ascites-only and vascular-graft-age wording needs current procedure-specific verification before becoming an order. [S7 §4; review limit]

## Safe examination sequence

1. **Position and monitor.** Left lateral position is typical; use appropriate sedation, pulse oximetry, BP/ECG monitoring, and capnography according to the sedation setting. Assess airway risk before entry. [S7 §§4–5]
2. **Pass the inlet under direct vision.** Advance over the tongue, recognize laryngeal anatomy, and enter the esophageal lumen gently. Avoid blind force against resistance or a diverticular pouch. [S7 §§3,5]
3. **Inspect the esophagus.** Describe mucosa, narrowing, rings, tears, varices and masses; record Z-line, GE-junction and diaphragmatic landmarks rather than collapsing them into one label. [S7 §§3,5]
4. **Clear and inspect the stomach.** Suction fluid/blood/retained material, use enough insufflation to see without unnecessary overdistension, and inspect the body, incisura and antrum. [S7 §§5–6]
5. **Retroflex.** Inspect the cardia, fundus and proximal stomach systematically; a forward view alone misses relevant proximal surfaces. [S7 §5; S5 §1]
6. **Traverse the pylorus under vision.** Inspect the bulb, then D2; further D3 inspection is described in SCORE when feasible. Recognize the medial ampullary region without assuming a routine forward-view exam gives an ERCP-quality papillary assessment. [S7 §§3,5; editorial scope qualification]
7. **Withdraw deliberately.** Reinspect, obtain indicated labeled samples, document findings/therapy and remove excess gas to reduce discomfort. [S7 §§5–6]

In active hemorrhage, complete inspection must be balanced against airway, hemodynamic and visualization limits. Document what was not seen and why rather than describing an incomplete examination as normal. [S7 §§4–6; editorial safety synthesis]

## Biopsy and therapy decisions

Biopsy suspicious mucosal lesions and gastric ulcers when appropriate; record the site for each specimen and reconcile the pathology with the visual finding. A nonspecific “gastric biopsy” label can make a targeted abnormality impossible to follow. In the esophagus, the channel's parallel approach to the wall may require deliberate tip positioning to obtain tissue safely. [S7 §6; S1 §5.2]

| Finding | Decision |
|---|---|
| Actively bleeding ulcer or visible vessel | Use lesion-directed hemostasis and the [Forrest pathway](upper-gastrointestinal-bleeding.md#forrest-classification-and-treatment-implications); epinephrine alone is inadequate definitive therapy. [S12 Endoscopic hemostasis] |
| Adherent clot | Follow the updated [UGIB clot guidance](upper-gastrointestinal-bleeding.md#forrest-classification-and-treatment-implications); removal requires ability to control the underlying lesion. [S9 Table 1 p.905] |
| Esophageal varix in the bleeding pathway | Band ligation with appropriate vasoactive/antibiotic treatment, not ulcer cautery. [S5 §7] |
| Suspicious gastric ulcer/mass | Tissue diagnosis and a documented pathology/follow-up plan. [S7 §6; S1 §5.2] |
| Stricture or foreign body | Reassess whether the planned technique, airway and equipment match the lesion; technical dilation/retrieval protocols need dedicated source guidance. [S7 §§2,6] |

Do not thermal-ablate an indeterminate lesion merely because small lesions can be treated through the channel. Histology, depth and lesion-specific oncologic criteria determine whether biopsy, resection, ablation or referral is appropriate; the supplied general module is not an oncologic resection protocol. [S7 §6; editorial qualification]

## Troubleshoot a poor view

Blood/clot or retained contents may require irrigation, suction, a larger/dual-channel scope, retrieval devices, patient repositioning, or an appropriately selected preprocedure prokinetic. Excess mucus/bubbles may be cleared with irrigation and selected agents such as simethicone. Wait out transient hypermotility when reasonable; equipment setup should be understood before an urgent examination. [S7 §6]

Altered anatomy, a J-shaped stomach, or looping may improve with reduced insufflation, patient repositioning or external pressure. Persistent resistance is a reason to reassess the view and anatomy, not to increase blind force. If safe visualization cannot be obtained, state the limit and arrange the next diagnostic/hemostatic step. [S7 §§5–6; procedural synthesis]

## Complications and rescue

Dental/oropharyngeal trauma, bleeding, perforation and sedation/airway events are important risks; tissue injury and stricture can follow selected interventions. New significant abdominal/chest pain, tachycardia, dyspnea, neck crepitus or peritoneal findings after EGD require evaluation for perforation or another complication. Not every perforation produces obvious pneumoperitoneum. [S7 §§1,7; S16 p.389]

For a stable patient with suspected duodenal perforation, CT/appropriate contrast imaging can define the defect and contamination. Selected small contained injuries may be managed with bowel rest, antibiotics, drainage and/or expert endoscopic closure. **Instability, a large defect, or uncontrolled contamination requires urgent surgical source-control assessment.** Repair/resection depends on location and tissue; pancreatoduodenectomy is an exceptional rescue consideration, not the default after EGD. [S7 §7]

When a complication is recognized, document the intervention, physiology and contamination assessment and involve the appropriate endoscopic/surgical team promptly. The full dose/route/timing of sedation reversal, aspiration management, anticoagulant reversal and post-perforation care is outside the supplied EGD module and remains a protocol gap. [S7 §§4,7; editorial handoff synthesis]

## Recovery and follow-up

Continue observation appropriate to the sedation and intervention; reassess new pain, bleeding and respiratory symptoms before discharge. The report should identify extent of examination, landmarks, findings, specimen sites, hemostasis/other therapy, complications, limitations, medication plan, and responsibility for pathology follow-up. This is an editorial handoff checklist built from the procedural and decision-making objectives. [S7 §§4–7; editorial synthesis]

For gastric ulcers, arrange pathology review and healing/malignancy follow-up. For H. pylori, use the [PUD cure-confirmation plan](peptic-ulcer-disease.md#treat-h-pylori-and-document-cure). For ulcer hemorrhage, use the [post-hemostasis PPI and rescue plan](upper-gastrointestinal-bleeding.md#endoscopic-hemostasis-and-acid-suppression). A completed scope is not the end of management. [S1 §§5–7; S12 Postendoscopic management]

## Board pearls

- **Z-line, proximal gastric folds and diaphragmatic pinch are separate landmarks.** [S7 §5; S2 p.391]
- The cardia/fundus need retroflexed inspection. [S7 §5]
- Diagnostic biopsy risk is different from dilation, ligation or PEG risk. [S7 §4]
- Protect the airway selectively in major bleeding; do not intubate every UGIB automatically. [S9 Table 1 p.904]
- Document biopsy sites and close the pathology loop. [S7 §6]
- New pain/tachycardia after instrumentation raises a perforation concern even without free air. [S7 §7; S16 p.389]
